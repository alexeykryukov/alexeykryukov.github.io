Playwright / Test Maintenance Case Study

What I learned maintaining 1000+ Playwright scenarios

On a UK booking product, I worked as the sole QA across web, iOS and Android. Over time, the Playwright suite grew to more than 1000 automated scenarios. The hard part was not writing the first tests. It was keeping a large suite useful.

The situation

The product had booking flows across web and mobile applications, and I was responsible for quality as the sole QA. Automation became an important part of keeping regression under control.

As the product grew, the Playwright suite also grew. Eventually it passed 1000 scenarios. At that size, a test suite becomes a product of its own: it needs structure, maintenance and good decisions.

More tests also create more maintenance

It is easy to celebrate a large test count, but every automated scenario has a cost. It can break because the product changed, because test data changed, because a selector became unstable, or because the scenario itself is no longer worth running.

A large automation suite is useful only if the team can trust the result. “1000 tests” is not a success metric by itself.

What mattered in practice

  • Keeping important business flows covered
  • Using reusable setup instead of repeating the same preparation in every test
  • Separating real regressions from flaky automation failures
  • Keeping scenarios readable enough to debug when something failed
  • Removing or fixing tests that stopped providing useful signal
  • Using automation together with manual and exploratory testing

Why sole-QA ownership changed the way I automated

Because I also had to test new features, investigate problems and support releases, I could not spend all my time maintaining automation.

That forced me to think about value. A test that takes a long time to maintain but rarely catches anything important can be worse than having no automated test for that scenario.

What I learned

Large Playwright suites need the same things as production code: structure, ownership, debugging, cleanup and regular decisions about what should stay.

My main lesson was simple: automation should make releases easier. If the suite itself becomes the biggest source of noise, something has gone wrong.

The bigger picture

Working across web and mobile also helped me avoid thinking about QA as a collection of isolated test cases. The real product flow can cross different clients, APIs and backend state.

That is why I prefer full QA ownership: automation is one part of quality, not the whole job.
