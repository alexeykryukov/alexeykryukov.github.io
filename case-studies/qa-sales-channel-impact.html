QA / Business Impact Case Study

How QA uncovered defects blocking sales channels

While working as a QA Lead on a healthcare / telemedicine product, I found defects that were preventing several sales channels from working correctly. After the fixes, sales through those channels increased by around 500%, representing roughly $500k in business impact.

Several sales channels affected by the defects
~500% sales increase through those channels after the fixes
~$500k rough business impact associated with the recovery

The situation

The product had multiple sales channels, and some of them were not working as expected. The issue was important because it was not just a cosmetic bug or a small usability problem: it directly affected whether customers could successfully move through those channels.

From a QA point of view, this was exactly the kind of problem where looking only at isolated screens or single test cases would not be enough. The full flow and the business outcome mattered.

What I found

During testing and investigation, I identified defects that were blocking several of those sales channels.

The important part was not only reporting that something failed. It was connecting the defect to the actual customer journey and making the business impact visible enough that the problem could be treated with the right priority.

A QA issue becomes much more useful to a team when it explains not only what is broken, but what that breakage means for users and for the business.

Why this mattered

In many products, a bug can look small technically while having a much larger effect on revenue, onboarding, payments or conversion.

This case was a good reminder that QA should not stop at “the feature works” or “the test passes”. The real question is whether the complete user and business flow works the way the product expects.

The result after the fixes

After the defects were fixed, sales through the affected channels increased by around 500%.

The estimated business impact was roughly $500k.

I do not describe this as “QA saved $500k”, because the result came from the whole team: the defects had to be understood, fixed and released. My contribution was identifying the problems that were blocking the channels and helping make their impact clear.

What I took from this

The best QA work is often not about the number of bugs found.

It is about finding the bugs that actually matter.

That can mean looking beyond the visible UI, understanding the full product flow, asking what the user is trying to achieve, and thinking about the business consequence when that flow breaks.

My role at the time

I was working as a QA Lead on a healthcare / telemedicine platform. My broader responsibilities included test strategy, automation with Selenium and C#, API testing, database validation, multiple test environments, team leadership and release decisions.

That wider product context helped me treat QA as more than execution of predefined test cases.
